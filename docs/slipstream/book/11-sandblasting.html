11. The Sandblasting Era

Estimated reading time: 14 minutes

1-minute summary

In 2022, the Zcash mainnet was flooded with spam transactions that packed up to 2.5 million outputs into 10,000-block windows. A sync engine that fetches blocks in fixed-count chunks hit a wall: one chunk decoded to over 1 GB of data, its 120-second timeout could never pass, and every retry restarted from scratch. Slipstream replaced fixed-count chunking with byte-budgeted splitting: each plan chunk is divided into sub-chunks of at most 8 MiB of wire bytes, each sub-chunk gets its own timeout, and retries resume from the last completed sub-chunk rather than restarting the whole plan chunk. The field acceptance run traversed 84,964 sandblasted blocks and 43.2 million outputs at ~8.9k blocks/minute with six server EOF cuts, all resume-recovered, zero timeout loops.

The field moment

The failure appeared in a birthday-935,000 wallet restore on a Mac-class machine. The sync proceeded normally through most of the chain history, then stalled completely around heights 1,735,000–1,785,000 (plan chunk indices 80–84 of the Historic range). The device log showed one line repeating forever:

chunk fetch timed out; retrying

No progress. No completion. No eventual success. Just an infinite retry loop.

The root cause was a collision of three properties: the 2022 spam era had packed the chain with an extraordinary number of outputs per block; the fetch pipeline fetched in fixed 10,000-block plan chunks; and the 120-second whole-chunk timeout was measured against the time to download and decode the entire chunk — not individual sub-pieces. One 10,000-block chunk in the spam region carried 2,536,689 outputs and decoded to approximately 1,032 MB of wire data. A 120-second timeout against a gigabyte of data over a real mobile network was structurally impossible to meet. And each retry restarted from the beginning of the same plan chunk, which meant no forward progress was ever made.

The 2022 spam era: density numbers

The Zcash network's Sapling protocol made it cheap to create outputs with minimal on-chain cost. In 2022, a spam campaign exploited this, injecting millions of outputs into consecutive blocks. The density peaked in roughly the 1.70M–2.00M block range on mainnet:

Field measurement

The 1,032 MB figure is from the Mac acceptance run (STATE.md, T6.8-S session log, 2026-06-12): a 10k-block chunk in the spam region split into up to 131 sub-chunks carrying up to 1,032 MB of plan-chunk wire data in total. This was observed at the exact heights that had been causing the infinite retry loop.

Why fixed block-count chunking is unfixable

The problem is not just that some chunks are slow to download. There are three structural failures with fixed block-count chunking against a variable-density chain:

  1. Timeout can never pass. A 120-second timeout on a gigabyte of data over a mobile connection is not a tuning problem — it is a physical impossibility given real mobile bandwidths. Raising the timeout to 600 seconds does not help: the next spam-era run will hit a chunk that takes longer.
  2. Retries restart from scratch. When the timeout fires, the partial download is discarded. The next attempt starts from the beginning of the same plan chunk. If the chunk's size is the problem, the retry will hit the same wall. The only exit is success, and success is impossible by the above point.
  3. Single-chunk RAM. Even if the download somehow completed, decoding and holding 2.5 million outputs in RAM simultaneously exceeds the memory budget of 2 GB devices (A10-class iPhones, iPad Air 2). Device jetsam would kill the process before scanning could begin.

The design: byte-budgeted splitting

The fix is density-adaptive chunking with no hardcoded heights. Instead of one plan chunk = one queue chunk, each plan chunk is split into sub-chunks of at most chunk_split_bytes bytes of estimated wire data (default 8 MiB, configurable via EngineConfig::chunk_split_bytes and the CLI --chunk-split-bytes flag).

Normal-era 10k-block chunks (1–6 MB) fit entirely within the 8 MiB budget and produce exactly one sub-chunk per plan chunk — identical to the pre-split behaviour. Spam-era chunks are divided into as many sub-chunks as needed to keep each below the budget.

The ChunkSplitter

The ChunkSplitter (fetch.rs:137–169) is a streaming accumulator. It receives blocks one at a time from the gRPC stream and yields a completed sub-chunk whenever the next block would push the accumulated size over split_bytes. A single block larger than the budget forms its own sub-chunk (no infinite loop). The splitter never yields empty sub-chunks and preserves stream order.

The (plan_index, sub_index, is_last) ordering key

The reorder stage needs to release sub-chunks from multiple workers in strict order. The key is extended from a single plan_index to a triple (plan_index, sub_index, is_last). The sub_index is dense from 0 and survives retries — if a worker resumes from height H after a partial download, its next sub-chunk carries the continuation of the pre-retry sequence. is_last marks the plan chunk's final sub-chunk so the reorder stage knows when to advance to the next plan index.

Deadline reset on progress

The 120-second deadline now bounds the accumulation of ONE sub-chunk (at most 8 MiB), not the entire plan chunk. The deadline resets whenever a sub-chunk is emitted (fetch.rs:366: last_progress = tokio::time::Instant::now()). A healthy-but-huge spam-era chunk delivers sub-chunks continuously — each sub-chunk resets the deadline, so the total stream time can far exceed 120 seconds without triggering a timeout. Only a genuinely stalled stream (no new messages for 30 seconds, via grpc::STREAM_IDLE_TIMEOUT) triggers an error that feeds into the resume-from-height retry.

Resume-from-height retry and the double-scan impossibility argument

When a plan chunk's download fails partway through, the PlanChunkCursor tracks the last height successfully emitted. On retry, the worker opens a new gRPC stream starting at resume_from (the height after the last emitted sub-chunk's last block). Already-emitted sub-chunks are never re-sent to the reorder stage — their heights have already been released to the ChunkQueue and consumed by the scanner. Double-scan is therefore structurally impossible: a height can only be emitted from the reorder stage once (the reorder stage verifies continuity and never re-accepts an already-seen height).

The resume point advances only on successful emission (the emit_sub_chunk function updates cursor.resume_from after a successful channel send — fetch.rs:289). A failed send does not advance the cursor.

AheadGate memory bounding

Without admission control, K−1 workers could each be streaming a ~300–900 MB spam-era plan chunk simultaneously, parking all of it in the reorder buffer while the floor (plan index 0) is being drained. On four fetch workers, this would be up to 3 GB in the reorder buffer — well beyond device memory.

The AheadGate (fetch.rs:193–229) bounds this. It allows non-floor workers to run at most 8 × split_bytes (= 64 MiB by default) ahead of the current release floor. The floor plan chunk streams unthrottled. Workers ahead of the floor must acquire byte-unit permits before emitting a sub-chunk; if the budget is exhausted, they pause until permits are released (which happens when the reorder stage drains buffered sub-chunks into the ChunkQueue). In normal-era operation, the 64 MiB budget admits at least 8 normal-size chunks in flight — no throttling occurs.

Progress display during the spam era

One known UX issue: progress percentage during the spam era shows as a very slow crawl. This is because the progress counter is block-based (blocks scanned / total blocks in pass), and a spam-era 10k-block chunk with 2.5 million outputs takes much longer to scan than a normal 10k-block chunk with 5,850 outputs — but both count as 10,000 blocks of progress. This is expected behaviour and is noted in STATE.md as a pending improvement (block-based vs. time-based progress during the spam era). The important thing is that the sync eventually completes, which it now does.

Device memory caveat (A10-class)

Known limitation — A10-class device memory

The Mac acceptance run observed RSS growing from 1.31 GB to 1.84 GB across the spam-era window. This reflects the 256 MB wire-queue budget × decode amplification + allocator high-water mark + per-range in-memory sparse shardtree. This is within budget on Mac (16+ GB RAM) and on newer iPhones (A15+, 6 GB RAM). However, A10-class devices (2016 iPad Air / iPhone 7, 2 GB RAM) may hit jetsam during the spam era. Per STATE.md T6.8-S: "DEVICE memory budgets need spam-era tuning before A10-class devices cross 1.7M". This is a known gap recorded in the roadmap (chapter 19). The device gate was not closed at commit ad755e08.

Why heights are not hardcoded

An alternative design would be to hardcode "split at height 1,700,000" or embed a density table. This was explicitly rejected: any hardcoded height range is specific to mainnet and breaks on testnet; future spam attacks would require code changes; and the density-adaptive approach handles both without modification. The chunk_split_bytes parameter is the only knob needed, and it can be tuned per device class without touching the engine logic.

Known limitations

Honest gaps
  • A10-class (2 GB) device memory tuning pending: the spam-era sync is functional on Mac-class and A15+ devices but has not been validated on 2 GB devices. Chapter 19 records this as an open item.
  • Block-based progress crawls through the spam era: progress percentage advances at the block rate, which is severely slow when each block carries tens of thousands of outputs. Expected UX for now.
  • No pre-seeded density hints: the splitter measures density live as it streams. For a hypothetical future spam attack at new heights, the engine adapts automatically — but the first encounter of each new dense region may produce temporary backpressure while the AheadGate limits ahead-workers.
